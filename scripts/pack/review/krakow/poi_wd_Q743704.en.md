## teaser
Welcome to Wawel Hill, with the royal castle and the cathedral where Polish monarchs were crowned and buried.
It is the most historically and culturally significant site in Poland.
## full
You're standing on Wawel Hill, the most historically and culturally significant site in Poland.
The castle and the cathedral sit on a limestone outcrop on the bank of the Vistula River.

This is where the Royal Route ends.
The kings and the coronation processions that came in through Saint Florian's Gate travelled up Castle Street to Wawel Castle.

The castle was established on the orders of King Casimir the Great.
For centuries, it was the residence of the kings of Poland and the symbol of Polish statehood.

Wawel Cathedral, nearly a thousand years old, stands directly beside the royal castle.
Polish monarchs were crowned and buried there, and it holds the relics of Saint Stanislaus.
In its Sigismund Tower hangs the Sigismund Bell, cast in 1520, one of the national symbols of Poland.
The bell weighs almost 13 tonnes, and it takes 12 bell-ringers to swing it by hand.

When Poland lost its independence in the late 18th century, Wawel became a symbol of endurance.
In 1978, Wawel was declared the first World Heritage Site, as part of the Historic Centre of Kraków.

Take a last look at the cathedral, and at the Sigismund Tower, home of the great bell.
## deep
Wawel's oldest stone buildings can be traced back to 970.
The hill also holds the earliest examples of Romanesque and Gothic architecture in Poland.
Some of the oldest remains are those of the Rotunda of the Virgin Mary.
The castle you see was built in the 14th century.
Over the next hundreds of years, it was enlarged into a number of buildings around a Renaissance courtyard.

The limestone of the hill abounds in caves, and one of them is the Dragon's Den.
At the foot of the hill, in front of the den, stands a dragon statue, installed in 1972.
It can let out fire from its mouth, and usually does so every 15 minutes.
One theory links the name Wawel to a Polish word that means ravine.
A ravine once divided the hill.
Archaeological remains show settlement on the hill from the 4th century.
In the 10th century, the lands of the Vistulans and Kraków became part of the emerging state of Poland.

For most of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, Wawel was the seat of the national government and the Diet, the assembly.
As the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth formed and grew, Wawel became the seat of one of the largest states in Europe.
That status was lost when the capital was moved to Warsaw in 1596.
From the late 18th century, Wawel was the setting for gatherings of people from Kraków, protesting against the foreign occupation.

Sigismund's Chapel, a royal chapel of the cathedral, was built from 1519 as a funerary chapel for the last members of the Jagiellonian dynasty.
In the crypt of the national bards, beneath the cathedral, lies the poet Adam Mickiewicz, whose monument stands in the Main Market Square.

The castle is now one of the country's premier art museums, established in 1930.
Its collections include Italian Renaissance paintings and the tapestries of Sigismund Augustus.
Its oriental art includes the largest collection of Ottoman tents in Europe.
In 2025, it was the most visited art museum in Poland.
