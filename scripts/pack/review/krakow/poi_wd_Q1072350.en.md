## teaser
In the centre of the Main Market Square stands the Cloth Hall, once a major centre of international trade.
Travelling merchants met here to discuss business and to barter.
## full
You're standing at the Cloth Hall, the central feature of the Main Market Square.
It was once a major centre of international trade.
Travelling merchants met here to discuss business and to barter.

In its golden age, in the 15th century, the hall was the source of exotic imports from the east: spices, silk, leather and wax.
Kraków itself exported textiles, lead and salt.

The Gothic hall burned down in 1555.
When it was rebuilt, it was crowned with an arcaded parapet, decorated with carved masks.
The building you see dates to the Renaissance, and it is one of the most recognizable monuments of the city.
In the 19th century, its successful renovation was one of the most notable achievements of a period of revival.

On the upper floor is a branch of the National Museum, with the largest permanent exhibit of 19th-century Polish painting and sculpture.
And the hall is still used as a centre of commerce today.

Before you walk on, find the carved masks along the parapet on the roof line.
## deep
The first cloth hall here was a double row of stone stalls, with a little lane down the middle.
At night, that lane was locked at both ends.
The prince had promised these stone stalls as early as 1257, when the town was founded.
Around 1300, the passage between the stalls was roofed, and it began to look like a market hall.
Before 1358, the king built a new Gothic hall here, 108 metres long.

In 1601, a passage was cut across the hall.
Between 1875 and 1879, it was rebuilt once more, and the stalls and buildings around it were pulled down.
Some of the carved masks on the gables are caricatures of two mayors of Kraków, made to a design by Jan Matejko.

The gallery upstairs opened on October 7, 1879.
Among its paintings are Prussian Homage by Jan Matejko and Nero's Torches by Henryk Siemiradzki.

Kraków was the capital of Poland, and its decline began when the capital moved to Warsaw at the end of the 16th century.
The hall has hosted many distinguished guests, among them Emperor Akihito of Japan, who was welcomed here in 2002.
In 1809, balls were held here after Prince Poniatowski had briefly liberated the city from the Austrians.

Since 2010, an entrance in the hall leads to an underground museum beneath the square.
There, visitors walk on glass ramps and footbridges above the old roads, including the paving of the medieval cloth hall.
Before you leave, notice the historic gas lighting along the outside of the hall, which still works.
