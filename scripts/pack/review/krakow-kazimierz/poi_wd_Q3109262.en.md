## teaser
This is the Izaak Synagogue, completed in 1644 and named after its donor, a banker to the king.
Today it is a practicing Orthodox synagogue once again.
## full
You're standing at the Izaak Synagogue, completed in 1644 in the Baroque style.
It is named after its donor, Izaak Jakubowicz, called Isaac the Rich, a banker to King Ladislaus of Poland.

Its founding legend was first told in the early 19th century by a Polish rebbe, Simcha Bunim of Peshischa.
In the legend, a poor and pious man named Ayzik dreams of a treasure hidden under the old bridge in Prague.
He travels there, but soldiers guard the bridge, and he tells their officer about his dream.
The officer has had a dream too: a treasure in the oven of a poor Jew in Kazimierz, named Ayzik.
Ayzik returns home, takes his oven apart, and finds the treasure.
The tale ends with a lesson: some things you look for the world over are found in your own home.

On 5 December 1939, the Gestapo ordered Maximilian Redlich, a Jewish official on duty at the Judenrat, to burn the scrolls of the Torah.
He refused, and he was shot dead.
The Nazis destroyed the interior and furnishings of the synagogue, including the bimah and the Aron Kodesh.
After the war, the building served as workshops and storage, and later as an exhibition space.
In 1989, it was returned to the Jewish community.
It is now a practicing Orthodox synagogue once again.

Look at the women's gallery and the outside stairs that lead up to it, a later addition to the building.

## drafting notes
Sensitive stop (docs/research/new-tours.md §2.2). The founding story is presented as a legend, with its first teller, as the source does; it is part of the synagogue's own tradition, not trivia. The murder of Maximilian Redlich is told in the source's words. The en article places the order at the Kraków Judenrat building; the pl article says Redlich was shot in front of the entrance of this synagogue ("rozstrzelany przed wejściem do bożnicy"). The script names no place, and does not say the scrolls were this synagogue's (the research doc's "Izaak Torah scrolls" wording was not used). The en article calls the building a practicing Orthodox synagogue again; the pl article describes its use after 1997 (a Jewish education centre, concerts) in the past tense, so a reviewer should confirm the present use. Left out: the officer's insult about Polish Jews, the diocesan objection that the design was "too beautiful for Jews" (it needs context the source lacks), the 1981 fire, the "most architecturally important" quote. "Practicing" follows the source's spelling.
