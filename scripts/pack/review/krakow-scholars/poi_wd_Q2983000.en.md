## teaser
This Neo-Gothic building is the Collegium Novum, the main building of the Jagiellonian University.
It opened in 1887, for the 500th anniversary of the university's foundation.
## full
You're standing at the Collegium Novum, the New College, the administrative centre of the Jagiellonian University.
Its architect designed it to match the oldest building of the university.
It opened on June 14, 1887, for the 500th anniversary of the university's foundation.
It replaced an old academic boarding school called Jeruzalem, which fire had consumed in the mid-19th century.

Inside is the great assembly hall, called the Aula.
Even on opening day, it was too small for all the guests.
Until the end of the First World War, a portrait of the emperor Franz Joseph hung there.
On October 31, 1918, a group of students tore it to pieces, to show their will for an independent Poland.

Upstairs, a lecture hall carries a plaque that recalls a darker day.
On November 6, 1939, the Nazi occupiers took the university's professors away from that hall.
In this action, called Sonderaktion Krakau, 183 professors were arrested and later sent to camps in Sachsenhausen and Dachau.

Before you walk on, look up at the Neo-Gothic front, restored in 1994.
## deep
The decision to pay for this building was made in Vienna, the Austro-Hungarian capital.
There, the university's interests were defended by the Austrian finance minister, Julian Dunajewski.
The work might not have begun at all without him and his brother, Cardinal Albin Dunajewski.

At the opening, the number of students did not exceed 1200, with about one hundred professors.
A debate arose over whether professors' wives should be invited to the grand ceremonies.
Most academics, in keeping with the trend of the time, were against it.
One professor wrote that he wished the ceremony to be exclusively male.

A number of other paintings in the Aula did survive.
There are portraits of the founding fathers, Casimir the Great and Władysław Jagiełło, and a picture of Queen Jadwiga painted in 1900.
There are works by Jan Matejko too, including his painting of Copernicus in conversation with God.
The chairs of the hall were designed by Tadeusz Stryjeński.
The hall was modernised, and the work was completed in 1999.
