## teaser
This is the Bishop's Palace, where Karol Wojtyła lived before he became Pope John Paul II.
At night he talked to the crowds from the window above the entrance.
## full
You're standing at the Bishop's Palace, the traditional home of the bishops of Kraków since the late 14th century.
It is the second largest palace in the city after Wawel.
Fire consumed it in 1462, and it was rebuilt.

Between 1958 and 1978, this was the home of Cardinal Karol Wojtyła.
In October 1978 he became the first Slavic Pope in history, taking the name John Paul II.
When he came back to the city, he stayed here.
At night he gave his blessings and talked to the people from the window above the main entrance.
That window is known as the papal window.

His bond with this house began much earlier.
During the war, as a student of the secret seminary of the archdiocese, he first lived in this complex.
After a Nazi roundup on August 6, 1944, which he narrowly escaped, the seminary students stayed in the palace until the Germans left the city.

After his death on 2 April 2005, some 40,000 people gathered here for a night vigil and prayer.
On each anniversary of his death, thousands of flowers are placed around the building.

Before you walk on, look up at the window above the entrance.
## deep
The palace stands across the street from the old Franciscan church.
Before it was built, the bishops of Kraków had their seat at Wawel, from the year 1000.
Between 1642 and 1647, Bishop Piotr Gembicki rebuilt it with a new staircase and rusticated portals.
It was renovated after the Swedish invasion of 1655.
In 1850 a fire that swept the whole city burned most of its furnishings and its collection of national mementoes.
Despite fires and the partitions of Poland, the palace always served its original purpose.

In the courtyard there is an arcaded loggia, added by the architect Gabriel Słoński around 1567.
There also stands a statue of John Paul II, sculpted and given to the palace by the artist Jole Sensi Croci in May 1980.

Karol Wojtyła was ordained a priest soon after the war, on November 1, 1946, by Cardinal Sapieha in his private chapel.
In March 2022, the archbishop welcomed refugees from the Russian invasion of Ukraine to live with him here.
